# Technique notes

How to do each move in a peer voice. Definitions follow the research codebook the skill was built
from, so the labels here mean the same thing they mean in the corpus statistics.

## Validation

Recognizing the person's emotions, thoughts, or behavior as understandable given their
situation. First instance is what counts; you do not need to keep validating every paragraph.

- Specific beats generic. Quote or paraphrase the exact thing: "dreading bedtime because you
  know the replay is coming" rather than "that sounds hard."
- Normalize when true: "you're not alone in this; it comes up here all the time."
- Empathize without taking over: "I'm sorry your doctor brushed you off" is validation;
  "here's what happened to me with my doctor" is not.
- Agreeing with an emotional complaint validates it. Agreeing with a factual opinion does not.
- Avoid: "at least...", "it could be worse", "everyone feels that way" said dismissively.

## Summarizing

Restating more than one of the person's points in your own words, as confirmation that you
heard them, sometimes to steer toward the pain point. "So not eating has become the one thing
that quiets the anxiety" or "on one hand you love your brother, on the other you're exhausted
and you want your own life." Do not add your opinion inside the summary; that comes next.

## Restructuring (reframing)

Helping the person shift perspective on an inaccurate or unhelpful belief. Pick one belief, the
one holding the most weight, and offer either an alternative view or a question that tests it.

Common distortions and a question for each:
- All-or-nothing ("I can't do anything"): what is one thing you did this week, however small?
- Catastrophizing ("this will ruin everything"): what is the most likely outcome, not the worst?
- Mind reading ("everyone thinks I'm weird"): what evidence do you actually have, and what
  else could their behavior mean?
- Fortune telling ("it will never get better"): has anything ever changed that you were sure
  wouldn't?
- Labeling ("I'm pathetic"): would you call a friend that for the same thing?
- "Should" rules ("I should be over this by now"): who wrote that deadline?
- Emotional reasoning ("I feel like a failure, so I am one"): feelings are real; are they
  accurate?
- Personalization ("it's my fault they're upset"): what else was going on for them?

Ways to phrase a reframe without lecturing:
- "Notice how the post goes from X to Y. That jump is the anxiety talking."
- "Another way to read that: ..."
- "It's pretty normal for people to ..., which doesn't make it about you."
- Metaphors: the pop-up ad you don't have to click; the smoke alarm that goes off for burnt
  toast; the tape the brain reviews for mistakes; the wave you ride instead of fight.

Restructuring versus telling someone their feelings are wrong: you are questioning the
conclusion ("therefore I'm worthless"), never the feeling ("I feel awful").

## Cognitive conceptualization (the "because")

Offering a hypothesis about why a thought, feeling, or behavior is happening: "avoidance
relieves the anxiety for an hour, which is exactly why the anxiety keeps growing." Useful in
small doses to make a suggestion make sense. Keep it as a theory ("it may be that"), and keep it
about mechanisms, not diagnoses.

## Psychoeducation

A brief, accurate fact about how minds and bodies work: fight-or-flight and why the heart races,
why fear fades with repeated exposure, what ERP is, why sleep deprivation amplifies anxiety.
Only when you are sure, and only a sentence or two. If you would not stake it on a psychology
textbook, leave it out. Never invent statistics.

## Recommendation

Suggesting something helpful for the first time, concretely. See the playbook. One to three per
reply; one done well beats five listed. Give the how, not just the what. If it is bad, unsafe, or
unverified, it is not a recommendation, it is bad advice.

## Grounding technique

A strategy to bring the body out of an alarm state in the moment. Give the steps:
- 5-4-3-2-1 senses; slow exhale-heavy breathing (4-7-8 or box 4-4-4-4); progressive muscle
  relaxation from feet to face; cold water on wrists or face; a slow walk while naming what you
  see; music that settles you.
- Grounding is for recovery from a spike (panic, an intrusive-thought surge, a shame spiral),
  not a daily chore. Say what it is for: "this won't delete the thought, it gets your body out
  of emergency mode so the thought loses volume."

## Journal / thought record

Writing things down to see them clearly. A thought record in one line: what happened, what
went through your mind, how strongly you believed it, how you felt, evidence for, evidence
against, a fairer thought, how you feel now. Or simpler: a worry slot, ten minutes with a
notebook before bed. Offer it as an experiment; some people find it feeds rumination.

## Behavioral activation (for depression)

The smallest action that can survive a bad day, scheduled rather than waited for: wash your
face, two minutes outside, one text to one person, one dish. Motivation follows action more
often than it precedes it; say that plainly, because it is the reason the tiny step matters.

## Exposure and small steps

Rank the feared situations from easiest to hardest; start with the one that is uncomfortable
but doable; repeat until it is boring; move up. Escaping at the peak teaches the brain the
danger was real; staying until the wave passes teaches the opposite. For social anxiety,
support groups and structured activities are practice with training wheels.

## Encouraging self-compassion

A recommendation to be kinder to yourself: "talk to yourself the way you'd talk to a friend in
this spot", "take whatever time it takes", "you're allowed to rest", "celebrate the step". Use
it whenever the post contains self-attack (pathetic, failure, weak, stupid, waste).

## Clinical referral

Recommending professional help, specifically and warmly, and always alongside something the
person can do now. Patterns that worked:
- "Tell your doctor the medication isn't working; adjusting it is what they're for."
- "ERP is the kind of therapy built for exactly this; it's worth asking for it by name."
- "If you're not ready for therapy, a CBT workbook or an online program is a real first step."
- "A regular GP visit is a fine place to start; you don't need to know what it is first."
- For caregivers: "a counselor for you, not just for him."
Acknowledge cost, waitlists, and bad past experiences instead of pretending they don't exist.

## Social support

Recommending time with other humans as support: one trusted person, a specific community, a
support group, family when family is safe. Not "be more social" but "who is one person you
could tell this to this week?"

## Acceptance

Making peace with a feeling, an urge, or an unchangeable fact, offered as a skill ("let the
urge be there and ride it out", "accept that you did that, neutrally, and move forward"). Never
acceptance of a distorted belief about oneself.

## Ending the reply

A question that invites the next message ("what does a typical night look like?", "what's been
the heaviest part?"), or a line of encouragement that is specific rather than generic ("the
fact that you wrote this out at 3am instead of spiraling alone is not nothing").
