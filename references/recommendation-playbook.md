# Recommendation playbook

What skilled peer supporters actually suggest, drawn from the responder sentences the research
team tagged Recommendation (about 1,100 in the corpus, of which only five were also flagged as
Bad Advice). Examples below are paraphrased, not quoted.

## What made these suggestions good

- **Specific to the post.** The single strongest pattern. Skilled peers rarely produced generic
  lists; they picked one or two things that fit the exact situation and explained how to do them
  ("write the worry down and read it out loud", "move bedtime ten minutes earlier at a time",
  "keep a comfort object in your pocket for the meeting").
- **Framed as options, not orders.** "Maybe", "you could", "have you considered", "one thing
  that helped a lot of people", "if you have the energy". Questions were common: "do you journal
  at all?", "could you do a video call with your therapist?"
- **Anchored in experience.** Humans often attached a suggestion to their own story ("what got
  me through was..."). You cannot truthfully do that, so anchor in the community instead: "a lot
  of people here found...", "something that comes up again and again in these threads is...".
- **Paired with another move.** Recommendation most often travelled with a reframe (restructuring
  first, then the suggestion that follows from it), with a grounding script, with a referral, or
  with a self-compassion nudge ("go gently with yourself while you try this").
- **Realistic about effort.** Good peers acknowledged that the suggestion is hard ("it will take
  some courage", "don't be discouraged when it doesn't work at first").

## By kind of suggestion

### Professional help (about 1 in 14 suggestions, almost always with something else)
- Name the kind of help: CBT for anxiety and depression, ERP (exposure and response prevention)
  for OCD and intrusive thoughts, CBT for insomnia, a psychiatrist or prescriber for medication
  questions, a counselor "for yourself" when the poster is a caregiver or sibling.
- Make it actionable and low-friction: "tell your doctor the medication isn't working, that's
  exactly the kind of thing they adjust", "could you get a video appointment", "a regular clinic
  or GP is a fine place to start", "online CBT programs exist if therapy isn't affordable yet".
- Acknowledge access problems honestly (cost, waitlists, a bad past therapist) instead of
  pretending they don't exist, and suggest the next-best step meanwhile (workbooks, support
  groups, a helpline).

### Grounding and breathing (about 1 in 12)
- 5-4-3-2-1: five things you can see, four you can touch, three you can hear, two you can smell,
  one you can taste. Give the steps, not just the name.
- Slow breathing with a longer exhale (4 in, 7 hold, 8 out, or box breathing 4-4-4-4).
- Body scan or progressive muscle relaxation, head to toe.
- Cold water on the face or hands, a short walk, music that brings you back to baseline.
- Frame grounding as a way to get through the spike, not as a cure: "it won't stop the thought,
  it gets your body out of alarm mode so you can think."

### Sleep, routine, basics (about 1 in 13; the most common suggestion type in anxiety threads)
- Bedtime moved earlier in small steps; a wind-down slot; screens and blue light off; a sleep
  story or quiet audio; expecting sleep to "come sooner or later" instead of forcing it.
- Regular meals, water, daylight, a walk; caffeine and alcohol as anxiety multipliers.
- For depression: the smallest routine that can survive a bad day (one meal, one shower, one
  walk), not a full schedule.

### Writing it down (about 1 in 25)
- Journaling feelings, then reading them back out loud; a thought record (situation, thought,
  feeling, evidence for and against, a fairer thought); a worry slot before bed; a note to your
  future self for the moments when motivation disappears.
- Peers were honest that journaling does not suit everyone ("it makes some people ruminate
  more"), so offer it as an experiment.

### Small steps and exposure (about 1 in 35)
- Break the frightening thing into steps you can actually take this week; start with the one
  that feels a 3 out of 10, not a 9. Ticking off small goals builds the evidence that the big
  thing is possible.
- Face the feared situation on purpose in a controlled way, repeatedly, and let the fear fade
  rather than escaping at the peak. Support groups double as safe practice for social anxiety.

### Challenging the thought (small share as a stand-alone suggestion, but the most common
### companion to other suggestions)
- Ask what the evidence is, what you would tell a friend who said this, what the realistic
  outcome is versus the catastrophic one, whether the thought is a fact or a feeling.
- Name the pattern lightly: catastrophizing, mind reading, all-or-nothing, "should" rules.
- Reframes peers used: "that's your mind doing its own thing, not a fact about you", "you can't
  control what people say, you can control what you take on board", "an intrusive thought is a
  pop-up ad, you don't have to click it", "it happens sometimes, it won't happen always".

### Movement (about 1 in 40)
- A walk even when you don't feel like it, running, sport, yoga; the point is a small win and a
  change of state, not fitness.

### Social support (about 1 in 23; dominant in caregiver and sibling threads)
- Reach out to one friend or family member and tell them what you're carrying; stay with
  trusted people when things are bad; specific support communities (peers named actual groups
  for their situation); a support group as a safe space.
- For caregivers: permission to have a life of your own, boundaries, respite, and that once you
  live independently you decide when and how much.

### Self-compassion and patience (about 1 in 37; highest in eating-disorder recovery threads)
- "Be very kind to yourself and take whatever time it takes", "you don't have to rush", "you're
  allowed a break from other people's stuff", "celebrate that step, it's bigger than it looks",
  "accept what you did neutrally instead of calling yourself a horrible person".

### Books, apps, resources (about 1 in 17; highest in CBT and recovery-focused threads)
- CBT and DBT skills workbooks (for anxiety, for depression), puzzle books for restless nights,
  guided meditations, sleep audio, structured online CBT programs. Only name a resource you are
  sure exists, and describe what it is for.

### Acceptance and letting go (about 1 in 23)
- "Sit with it", "let it pass, it will", "notice the urge and ride it out", "treat the thought as
  just a thought each time it returns and it slowly loses its grip". Acceptance is offered for
  feelings and urges, never for a distorted belief ("accept that you're unlovable" is not
  acceptance, it is surrender).

### Distraction and activities (about 1 in 11)
- Music, games, creative work, learning a skill, small projects that bring quiet satisfaction, a
  comfort object to hold in stressful settings. Peers framed these as ways through a wave, and
  as ways to rebuild a life that has more in it than the problem.

### Limiting triggers and boundaries (about 1 in 45)
- Time off social media during recovery; leaving the room when overwhelmed; boundaries with
  people who feed the problem; muting, unfollowing, deleting numbers when a relationship keeps
  reopening a wound.

## Which suggestions fit which community (dev-half tendencies, rounded)

- **Anxietyhelp:** sleep and routine changes, grounding, professional help, small exposures.
- **depression_help:** telling the doctor or psychiatrist when treatment isn't working,
  grounding and meditation, routine basics, movement, resources.
- **CBT / CBTpractice:** workbooks and resources, professional help, journaling and thought
  records, exposure and behavioral experiments, grounding.
- **getting_over_it:** resources and workbooks, professional help, small projects and skills.
- **socialanxiety:** graded steps, grounding, journaling, support groups as practice.
- **OCD:** routine, support communities, ERP, not engaging with the thought's content.
- **EatingDisorders / fuckeatingdisorders:** self-compassion, professional treatment, limiting
  triggers; no numbers.
- **siblingsupport / family caregiving:** social support and specific communities, counseling for
  the poster, boundaries and respite.
- **Phobia:** graded exposure, calming techniques, professional help.

## What the annotators flagged as bad advice (do not do these)

- Suggesting an AI chatbot as a substitute for therapy or a person.
- Promising a quick fix ("a few sessions of X will reset it").
- Prescribing suffering ("you just have to go through it").
- Comparing or recommending substances.
- Telling someone what to believe about themselves, or discouraging connection ("don't make
  friends in treatment").
- Medical claims that are false or unsourced (heart risk, biochemistry, what is "common").
- Asserting a diagnosis ("so yes, that is an eating disorder", "I assume you also have anxiety").
